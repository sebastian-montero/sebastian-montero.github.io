why engineering feels harder when coding is easy

19 November 2026

Software engineering has been turned on its head. It's been months since I wrote a single line of code, but there are still days where I feel like my job has become harder, more tiring and more challenging than it was before AI.

Maybe it is because we can do so much more in parallel and we can ship way faster. But this is all a side effect of a deeper cause. If you care about software engineering as a craft and the products you build, this exhaustion and restlessness come from dealing with ambiguity more often. Writing code was never really the job.

Writing software is just a tool to solve problems. The job is to use your ingenuity to deal with ambiguous situations and solve problems within a specific system while adhering to a set of constraints.

The software development process always starts with a problem or feature request. Even when the specification is already thought out, you, as an engineer, need to think about how this piece of work fits into a broader system (e.g., who are the consumers, what data are you dealing with, what is the expected performance, etc.). Once this is defined, you start building and testing, and finally ship.

Before AI, the time frames would be long. You could take time defining and scoping the problem, then spend weeks building and testing. I honestly think that writing code has always been the easy part of the job. It took time and there were lots of learnings involved, especially when working with new languages, tools and frameworks, but once you knew what you were building, you had a target and you just needed to move closer towards it.

AI changed the proportion of time within each stage. The time it takes to write code went down from weeks to hours. This means that you can now move on to the next problem faster. And this is why I think the job is now harder. Now, we deal with ambiguity way more often.

The process of speaking with people, understanding the problems, defining the architecture and making it fit within an existing system has become the bulk of the job. Dealing with unknowns is not easy, and you need to be tuned into the current system and find reasonable solutions that merge the demands of your stakeholders with what is already deployed.

I still believe that most software is rarely right on the first pass and iteration is inevitable, but you can't compromise on scoping the problem statement because it defines what you are solving and how your work fits into a broader ecosystem.

Even if agents write the code today, your job as a developer is to understand the system and its tradeoffs. You need to understand the risks and opportunities of what you are building, and you always need to understand what you are solving.
